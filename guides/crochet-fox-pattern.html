Free Crochet Fox Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A friendly stuffed fox with a pointed snout and tall ears, built from the same spiral shaping as our mini ball pattern and finished with a fluffy tail.

Updated 2026 · US terms · About 7in / 18cm tall

The first fox I ever made had a snout like a ping-pong ball. I was certain I'd followed the shaping perfectly, but when I sewed it onto the face my husband walked past and asked, very gently, why I was crocheting a cat. The problem wasn't the stitches — it was that I'd stopped the snout too wide and stuffed it round instead of letting it taper to a point. I unpicked it, worked the tip from a tiny ring of four stitches, and it suddenly read as a fox. That's the detail I want to save you from here: a fox is mostly a ball with a pointed snout and two tall ears, and once you know how to shape those two things, everything else falls into place.

This pattern is written for a beginner who already has a magic ring, a single crochet, and an increase under their belt. If the magic ring is still fighting you, our magic ring tutorial walks through it slowly, and our basic crochet stitches guide covers every stitch used here. Everything is worked in a continuous spiral in US terms, exactly the same way we build the mini amigurumi ball — so if you can make that, you can absolutely make this fox.

You'll end up with a fox around 7in / 18cm tall, though the body is forgiving and I'll show you how to scale it up or down further down the page. Take your time with the snout and the ear placement, and your fox will come out looking like a fox instead of a very confused cat.

Toy safety note: for children under 3, embroider the face with yarn instead of using plastic safety eyes. Stitch all seams tightly and check periodically for wear. See the FAQ below for more detail.
Pick your hook before you pick your yarn. Amigurumi relies on tight stitches so the stuffing never peeks through, which means a hook a size or two smaller than the ball band suggests. If you're unsure which yarn behaves best for toys, read our best yarn for amigurumi guide first.

Materials

  • 1 skein orange worsted-weight yarn (about 90–110 yards)
  • Small amounts of white/cream and black yarn for the snout, tail tip, and face
  • 3.5mm hook (smaller than standard, for tight amigurumi stitches)
  • Polyester fiberfill stuffing
  • Safety eyes, or embroidery thread for a child-safe embroidered face
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors and a yarn needle

Gauge

Gauge isn't critical — the goal is tight, even stitches that hide the stuffing. If you can see stuffing through your stitches, try a smaller hook.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral, same technique as our mini amigurumi ball pattern.
  • Made from seven separate pieces (head, body, snout, 2 ears, tail, 4 legs) sewn together at the end.
  • For a child under 3, skip plastic safety eyes and embroider the face instead.
  • If you're brand new to this style, read amigurumi for beginners before you start.

Pattern

Head, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Head, Rounds 5–9

Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping. (24 sts each round)

Head, Rounds 10–12

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the body.

Snout

Magic ring, 4 sc. (4) → Inc around. (8) → [sc 3, inc] around. (10) → Sc around, stuffing lightly at the tip and a touch more at the base. (10)

Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing to the face.

Ears (make 2)

Magic ring, 4 sc. (4) → [sc 1, inc] twice. (6) → [sc 2, inc] twice. (8) → Sc around. (8)

Fasten off, leaving a short tail for sewing on flat, without stuffing.

Body, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Body, Rounds 5–9

Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping. (24 sts each round)

Body, Rounds 10–12

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the head.

Tail

Magic ring, 4 sc. (4) → Inc around. (8) → [sc 1, inc] around. (12) → Sc around for 5 rounds. (12) → [sc 1, dec] around. (8) → Sc around, stuffing lightly. (8)

Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing. Add a couple of rounds of white at the tip for a classic fox tail if you like.

Legs (make 4)

Magic ring, 5 sc. (5). Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping, stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing.

Assembly

Sew the snout to the centre front of the head, and the two ears to the top of the head, checking placement from directly above so both sides match. Sew the body to the bottom of the head, the four legs to the body, and the tail to the lower back, angling it up. Attach safety eyes before stuffing the head if you're using them, or embroider a simple face with yarn once assembly is complete for a child-safe alternative.

Choosing yarn & colour for a fox

Foxes read as foxes mostly because of three colours: an orange body, a white chest and snout, and a black nose with dark ear tips. You don't need a huge palette — a single skein of orange, a little ball of white or cream, and a scrap of black will carry the whole project. If orange feels too loud for a nursery, a soft rust or terracotta works just as well and still reads unmistakably as a fox.

I've learned to check how the white behaves before committing to it. Some whites are a touch translucent, and when they're used for the snout that sits right at the front of the face, a pale orange can show through and muddy the look. If your white looks thin against the orange, work the snout with a slightly tighter tension or hold it double with a fine white thread. For the yarn itself, a worsted-weight acrylic is my honest recommendation for a first fox — it's cheap, forgiving, and machine-washable if the toy ever ends up in a toddler's mouth. Our best yarn for amigurumi guide runs through the trade-offs if you'd rather use cotton or a wool blend.

Sizing your fox

The pattern as written lands at about 7in / 18cm tall with a worsted yarn and a 3.5mm hook, which is a lovely, hold-in-one-hand size for a gift. But one of the most useful things to understand about amigurumi is that the shape is a recipe, not a fixed ruler. Because the increases happen on a predictable schedule, you can add or remove rounds without breaking the maths.

To make a bigger fox, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds, and one more decrease round at the end, then lengthen the straight rounds by a round or two so the proportions stay balanced. To go smaller, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds. Changing your hook size is the simplest lever of all: the same pattern with a chunkier yarn and a larger hook produces a visibly larger fox with no other adjustments, just looser stitches. Whatever you choose, keep the snout and ears in proportion — a giant head with a tiny snout is exactly how my first fox became a cat.

Sewing the pieces together

Assembly is where a fox becomes a fox, and it's also where beginners rush. I always tell my classes to lay every finished piece out flat on the table first, snout to the front, ears at the top, tail at the back, and just look at the arrangement for a minute before touching the needle. It sounds silly, but ten seconds of looking saves twenty minutes of unpicking.

Sew each piece on using the tail you left when you fastened off, threading it through a yarn needle and working small, tight stitches all the way around the attachment point rather than a few anchor stitches at the corners. Start with the head and body, then the snout, then the ears, and save the legs and tail for last so the fox can sit up and you can judge where each leg should sit for balance. The tail should angle up and slightly back — sew it on with the opening tilted upward rather than pointing straight down, and it will keep that jaunty fox look instead of drooping.

Gifting a handmade fox

A crochet fox is one of my favourite gifts because it reads as intentional in a way a plain ball doesn't, but it's still quick enough to finish before a birthday. If it's for a baby, embroider the face instead of using safety eyes, and include a small note telling the parents it's spot-clean only. If it's for an older child or an adult, a small accessory — a scrap-yarn scarf, a tiny chain-loop bow, or a contrast belly panel — turns a nice fox into a keepsake.

One thing I always do before wrapping is give the fox a gentle tug test: pull lightly on each ear, leg, and the tail to be sure nothing shifts. It's the same check I'd do for anything made with a magic ring, and it's caught more loose tails than I care to admit. If a piece moves, you'll be far happier catching it on your own kitchen table than watching a child discover it first.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: the snout comes out round and ball-like instead of pointed.
Fix: start the snout from a magic ring of only 4 sc and widen slowly, and stuff it lightly at the tip with a touch more at the base. A snout that's been stuffed firmly all over, or started too wide, loses its point.
Mistake: the ears end up sewn on unevenly or set too far back, which flattens the fox look.
Fix: lay the head flat and pin or mark both ear positions before stitching, checking from directly above. The ears should sit slightly forward on the crown, not at the very back.
Mistake: the tail droops or is sewn too low on the body.
Fix: position the tail opening so it angles up and back, and sew around the whole opening with tight stitches rather than a few loose anchors. Lighter stuffing also helps it hold its shape.
Mistake: the head wobbles on the body, leaving a visible gap at the neck.
Fix: use the long tails from both pieces and sew around the full circumference of the neck, going through the stitches of each piece so the join is snug before you tie off.
Mistake: white stuffing shows through the orange stitches, especially after the toy is handled.
Fix: drop down a hook size and tighten your tension, and stuff in small, even pieces rather than one big clump. If stuffing still shows, switch to a yarn with a tighter twist.
Mistake: safety eyes are placed too far apart or too close together, making the face look off.
Fix: insert the eye posts but don't lock the backings on yet, then hold the head up and check the spacing from a normal viewing distance before securing them. On a fox, the eyes sit just above and either side of the snout.

Frequently asked questions

Is this crochet fox pattern safe for young children?

For children under 3, embroider the face with yarn instead of using plastic safety eyes, since even securely attached safety eyes carry a small-parts risk for very young children. Stitch the ears, tail, and legs on firmly and check the seams regularly.

How long does the crochet fox take to make?

Most beginners finish this fox over two to three sessions, roughly 3 to 4 hours of crochet time plus assembly. The head and body are the slowest parts; the snout and the four legs are quick wins that make the project feel like it's moving.

What yarn colours do I need for a fox?

You need an orange main colour, a white or cream for the snout and tail tip, and a small amount of black for the nose and eyes. Worsted-weight acrylic in any of those shades works well.

How do I get the snout to look pointy instead of round?

Start the snout from a magic ring of only 4 single crochet and let it widen slowly, then stuff it lightly at the tip and a touch more at the base. A round snout usually means it was started too wide or stuffed too firmly at the point.

Can I make the fox bigger or smaller?

Yes. To scale up, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds and one more decrease round at the end. To scale down, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two.

How do I attach the tail so it doesn't droop?

Sew the tail on around the whole opening with small, tight stitches, and position it so it angles up and back rather than straight down. Stuff it lightly rather than firmly, which keeps it fluffy and easier to hold its shape.

Do I need to know the magic ring before I start?

Yes, every piece starts with a magic ring so the pieces close with no hole. If you are still getting the hang of it, practice it a couple of times before beginning the fox, or start with our magic ring tutorial.
