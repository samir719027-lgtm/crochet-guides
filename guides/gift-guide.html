A relative once gave me a huge cone of scratchy craft-store acrylic in a shade of orange I would never have chosen, clearly proud of finding "the crochet person" something on theme. That's the gap this guide is built to close.

Crochet gifts go wrong in one predictable way: buying yarn or a hook in a size, colour, or fibre the person never asked for. The safest gifts are the ones that work regardless of what someone is currently making.

What to avoid unless you know for sure

  • Yarn — unless you know their current project or a colour they've specifically mentioned, this is the most likely gift to go unused.
  • A single hook size — most crocheters already own the common sizes; a set is safer than one more of a size they probably have.
  • Pattern books in a style they don't make — amigurumi patterns for someone who only makes blankets, for example.

How much to spend

Crochet gifts scale easily to almost any budget, which makes this an easy category to shop well in without overspending. Small notions — stitch markers, a tapestry needle set, a hook grip — typically run $10–25 and make solid stocking-stuffer or add-on gifts. An ergonomic hook set or a well-made project bag sits in the $25–50 range and works as a standalone main gift. For a bigger occasion, a hook case, a ball winder, or a stitch dictionary in the $50-and-up range reads as a genuine investment in someone's hobby rather than an afterthought.

If you're choosing between two options at the same price, favour the one that's useful regardless of what the person is currently making over one tied to a specific project, colour, or skill level — it's the same logic behind avoiding yarn and single hooks, just applied to bigger purchases too.

Common gift-buying mistakes

Mistake: buying yarn in a colour or fibre that seemed nice in the store but doesn't match anything the person is working on.
Fix: ask what they're currently making, or check their project bag or Ravelry queue if you can, before choosing yarn — or skip yarn entirely and go with notions instead.
Mistake: buying a single hook in a size they probably already own.
Fix: choose a full ergonomic hook set instead, which covers sizes they'll need as they take on new projects rather than duplicating what's already in their case.
Mistake: choosing a gift pitched at the wrong skill level — an advanced stitch dictionary for someone still learning single crochet, or a beginner kit for someone with years of experience.
Fix: match the gift to what they actually make now: beginner kits and instructional bundles for someone newer, reference books and technique-expanding tools for someone experienced.

Frequently asked questions

What is the safest crochet gift if I don't know their preferences?

Notions like stitch markers, a project bag, or a yarn bowl are safe because they aren't tied to a specific hook size, yarn weight, or style preference, unlike yarn or a single hook.

Should I buy yarn as a gift for a crocheter?

Only if you know their current project or a colour palette they've mentioned. Most experienced crocheters already have yarn preferences and unrequested yarn often goes unused.

What's a good gift for someone who just started crocheting?

A beginner kit with an ergonomic hook, a stitch marker set, and a simple pattern book gives a new crocheter room to practise without assuming any preferences they haven't formed yet.

How much should I expect to spend on a crochet-related gift?

Useful notions and small tools typically run $10-25, an ergonomic hook set or project bag $25-50, and a nicer hook case or yarn winder $50 and up, so there's a workable option at most budgets.
