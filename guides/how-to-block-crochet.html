How to Block a Crochet Project (And When You Actually Need To)

Pin this pattern

What blocking does, the three common methods, and how to tell whether your project actually needs it.

Updated 2026 · 9 min read

For a long time I skipped blocking entirely, mostly because nobody explained what it was actually for, and the word made it sound like a whole separate skill I didn't have time to learn. Then I joined six granny squares that were each a slightly different size and shape, and spent an evening fighting a blanket that wouldn't lie flat. Blocking those squares first, even just with a quick spray and pin, would have saved me the entire evening.

Blocking is the step most beginner tutorials skip, and most finished projects would look better for. It's the process of dampening a finished piece and reshaping it flat while it dries, which relaxes uneven tension, straightens curled edges, and can flatten stitches into a neater, more even fabric.

Quick answer: block flat pieces like granny squares, blanket panels, and garment sections. Skip it for amigurumi, and be cautious with acrylic yarn, which barely responds to wet blocking.

When blocking actually helps

Blocking is worth doing for:

  • Granny squares before joining them — makes edges straighter and squares more uniform in size, covered in our granny square tutorial.
  • Garment pieces before seaming, so panels lie flat and match up correctly.
  • Lace or open-stitch patterns, where blocking opens up the stitch pattern and makes it visible.
  • Curled edges on single crochet pieces — also addressed in our common mistakes guide.

It's rarely worth it for amigurumi (stuffing already holds the shape) or heavily textured stitches, where flattening can actually hide the texture you were trying to show.

Three common methods

Most common

Wet blocking

Submerge the piece in cool water until fully damp, gently squeeze out excess (don't wring), then pin it flat to the finished dimensions on a blocking mat or towel. Leave to dry completely before unpinning. Works best on natural fibres like wool and cotton.

Fastest

Spray blocking

Pin the piece to shape first, then mist it with water from a spray bottle until damp, rather than fully submerging it. Gentler and faster than wet blocking, and a reasonable middle ground for pieces you don't want to fully soak.

For acrylic yarn

Steam blocking

Hold a steamer or iron on a steam setting a few centimetres above the pinned piece — never touch the iron directly to the fabric. This is the method that actually works on acrylic, since acrylic fibres respond to heat rather than water. Go slowly and check often; too much heat can flatten stitches permanently.

Table: which method for which yarn

FibreBest method
WoolWet or spray blocking
CottonWet or spray blocking
AcrylicSteam blocking (careful, low heat)
BlendsTest on a swatch first — behaviour depends on the dominant fibre

What you actually need

Blocking doesn't require a big kit. At minimum you need something to pin into and something rustproof to pin with: a foam blocking mat is worth owning if you block often, but a clean towel laid over carpet works for an occasional project. Rustproof T-pins hold shape without leaving marks or rust spots on damp fabric — regular sewing pins can leave a stain if left in too long.

A spray bottle filled with plain water covers spray blocking entirely. Blocking wires, which thread through the edge of a piece to keep straight lines perfectly straight, are a nice upgrade for garment panels but not something a beginner needs to buy before a first attempt.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: a piece gets stretched out of shape rather than improved.
Fix: this usually means it was pulled to fit a size larger than the pattern intended, rather than pinned to the actual finished measurements. Measure and pin to the pattern's stated dimensions, not as far as the damp fabric will physically stretch.
Mistake: acrylic yarn barely changes after wet blocking.
Fix: this isn't a technique error — acrylic fibres simply don't respond much to water the way wool and cotton do. Switch to steam blocking instead, holding the steamer a few centimetres above the fabric rather than touching it directly.
Mistake: an amigurumi or heavily textured piece loses its shape or texture after blocking.
Fix: both are usually better left unblocked. Stuffing already holds an amigurumi's shape, and flattening textured stitches like popcorns or bobbles under pins can press the texture flat instead of highlighting it.

Frequently asked questions

What does blocking actually do to a crochet project?

Blocking dampens the fibres and lets you reshape and flatten the piece as it dries, which relaxes uneven tension, straightens curled edges, and can adjust the finished dimensions slightly.

Do all yarn fibres block the same way?

No. Natural fibres like wool and cotton hold a blocked shape well. Acrylic yarn barely responds to wet blocking and needs steam blocking instead, done carefully, since heat can flatten or damage the stitches if overdone.

Does every crochet project need to be blocked?

No. Amigurumi and most toys are never blocked. Blocking mainly helps flat pieces like garment panels, blankets, and granny squares, where an even, flat finish matters for the final look or for joining pieces together.

What equipment do I actually need to block a crochet project?

At minimum, something to pin into (a foam blocking mat, or even a clean towel over carpet) and rustproof T-pins. A spray bottle covers spray blocking, and blocking wires are a nice-to-have for straight garment edges but not required for most beginner projects.

Can I over-block a crochet project?

Yes. Stretching a piece too aggressively to hit exact dimensions can permanently distort the stitches, especially on natural fibres that reshape easily when wet. Pin to the pattern's stated measurements rather than pulling as far as the fabric will physically go.
