Basic Crochet Stitches for Beginners: Complete Guide

Pin this pattern

Six stitches, in the order you'll actually need them, each explained step by step.

Updated 2026 · US terms · 13 min read

I remember sitting with my grandmother's old hook feeling completely convinced I was doing something wrong, because my chain kept twisting and my "stitches" looked nothing like the picture in the book she'd handed me. What nobody told me at the time is that almost none of that was actually a stitch problem — it was a tension problem, and tension comes from how you hold the yarn, not from the stitch itself. Once that clicked, every stitch on this page took about ten minutes each to learn.

Nearly every crochet pattern on this site, from our dishcloth pattern to our granny square blanket, is built from some combination of the six stitches below. Learn them roughly in this order, and you'll have everything you need for the vast majority of beginner patterns.

Quick answer: chain stitch and single crochet alone are enough to complete a real first project. The other four stitches add height and texture options as you're ready for them.

Holding your hook and yarn

Before the stitches themselves, the single biggest thing that makes crochet feel awkward at first is hand position, not skill. Hold the hook like a pencil, loosely, resting it in the crook between your thumb and index finger rather than gripping it in your fist — a tight grip is the single most common cause of stitches that are too tight to work into on the next row. For the yarn, most people find it easiest to weave it once over the index finger, under the middle finger, and over the ring finger of the hand not holding the hook, then let the index finger do the actual "yarn over" motion. There's no single correct way to do this — if your tension ends up even and your hand doesn't cramp, your grip is right.

The six basic stitches

01

Chain stitch

Abbreviation: ch

The foundation of almost every project. Yarn over the hook and pull it through the loop already on your hook. Repeat to build a chain of any length — this is usually the very first row you make.

02

Slip stitch

Abbreviation: sl st

Insert your hook into a stitch, yarn over, and pull the yarn through both the stitch and the loop on your hook in one motion. Slip stitches don't add height — they're mainly used to join rounds or move across a row without building fabric.

03

Single crochet

Abbreviation: sc

Insert your hook into a stitch, yarn over and pull up a loop (two loops now on your hook), yarn over again and pull through both loops. This is the shortest, densest stitch, and the one used throughout our dishcloth pattern.

04

Half double crochet

Abbreviation: hdc

Yarn over before inserting your hook into the stitch. Insert, yarn over and pull up a loop (three loops on your hook), yarn over once more and pull through all three loops at once. Slightly taller and softer than single crochet.

05

Double crochet

Abbreviation: dc

Yarn over before inserting your hook. Insert, yarn over and pull up a loop (three loops on hook), yarn over and pull through just two loops (two loops remain), yarn over again and pull through the final two loops. Taller still, with more drape.

06

Treble (triple) crochet

Abbreviation: tr

Yarn over twice before inserting your hook. Insert, yarn over and pull up a loop (four loops on hook), then yarn over and pull through two loops, three separate times, until one loop remains. The tallest of the six, used less often in beginner patterns.

Common tension mistakes

Stitches so tight you can barely get the hook back in. This is almost always a grip issue, not a stitch issue. Loosen how tightly you're holding the working yarn against your fingers, and check that you're not also gripping the hook itself like a fist around a bat.
Chain looks twisted instead of flat. This usually means the hook rotated slightly with each stitch. Keep the flat side of the hook facing up consistently as you work, and the chain will lie flat.
Row count keeps coming out wrong. The most common culprit is the very first chain of a new row: depending on the stitch, it may or may not count as your first stitch. Check the pattern's notes on turning chains before assuming you've dropped or added a stitch somewhere in the middle.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest crochet stitch to learn first?

Chain stitch is the very first stitch every crocheter learns, followed by single crochet. Together these two stitches are enough to complete an entire beginner project, like our dishcloth pattern.

What is the difference between single, half double, and double crochet?

The difference is height and the number of yarn overs used before completing the stitch. Single crochet is shortest and densest, half double crochet is a middle height, and double crochet is taller and drapes more loosely.

Do I need to learn treble crochet as a beginner?

Not right away. Treble crochet appears less often in beginner patterns than the shorter stitches, so it's fine to learn it later once single, half double, and double crochet feel comfortable.

Why is my crochet tension so uneven?

Uneven tension almost always comes from how tightly you hold the working yarn, not the hook. Try wrapping the yarn once around your pinky and letting it run over your index finger, which gives consistent, light resistance without you having to think about it.

What does "yarn over" actually mean?

It means wrapping the working yarn over the top of your hook from back to front, before you pull it through a stitch or loop. Almost every stitch in this guide is built from one or more yarn overs in a specific order.
