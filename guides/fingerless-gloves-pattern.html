Free Crochet Fingerless Gloves Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A snug tube with a thumb gap, sized for adult hands — the grown-up version of our kids mitts.

Updated 2026 · US terms · One adult size, adjustable

I designed the kids fingerless mitts pattern first, for my niece, then spent an entire winter jealous every time she showed off her matching mittens. Scaling it up for adult hands turned out to be almost embarrassingly simple — a bigger starting chain and a few extra rounds, and the exact same construction I'd already worked out fit me perfectly. These have been my go-to gloves for typing at a cold desk and knitting on the couch ever since, since the fingers stay completely free.

If you've made the kids version already, this pattern will feel instantly familiar. If you haven't, it's still a great first project: there's no fitted fingers to work individually, no complicated shaping, and only one new technique — the six-chain thumb gap — which this guide walks through in detail below.

These gloves use the same tube-with-a-thumb-gap construction as our kids fingerless mitts pattern, scaled up for an adult hand and wrist. No fitted fingers to work individually — just a snug tube that stretches comfortably into place.

Materials

  • 1 skein worsted-weight yarn (about 100–130 yards for a pair)
  • 4.5mm (US size 7) crochet hook
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle, for weaving in ends

Abbreviations (US terms)

Abbr.Means
chChain
scSingle crochet
st(s)Stitch(es)
rnd(s)Round(s)

See our abbreviations glossary for the full list including UK terms.

Gauge

14 sc and 16 rows measures about 4in / 10cm square with a 4.5mm hook and worsted yarn. Fit depends directly on your starting chain — measure your hand before starting for the best fit.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral — use a stitch marker to track round starts, same technique as our kids mitts pattern.
  • Makes one glove; repeat for a matching pair.
  • The numbers below fit an average adult hand; adjust the starting chain in multiples of 2 for a snugger or looser fit.

Pattern (make 2)

Round 1

Ch 28, join with a sl st to form a ring, being careful not to twist the chain. Place marker. (28 sts)

Rounds 2–7

Sc in every st around. (28 sts each round)

Round 8: thumb gap

Sc 22, ch 6 (skip next 6 sts, leaving a gap for the thumb), sc in remaining sts to end of round. (28 sts total, including the 6-chain gap)

Round 9

Sc in every st around, working sc into each of the 6 chain sts from the gap as if they were regular stitches. (28 sts)

Rounds 10–13

Sc in every st around. (28 sts each round)

Finishing

Fasten off and weave in ends with a yarn needle. Repeat the entire pattern for the second glove.

Choosing yarn and color

Worsted-weight is the friendliest starting point since gauge and hook sizing for it are well documented everywhere, but the fiber content changes how the finished gloves feel and function. A wool or wool-blend yarn traps warmth well, which makes sense for gloves worn outdoors or in a cold house; a cotton or cotton-acrylic blend breathes better and holds its shape nicely for gloves worn indoors at a desk or while doing other handwork.

For gifting, a single skein in a rich solid color reads as more polished than a busy variegated yarn, since the plain single crochet fabric doesn't have texture to compete with a print. If you'd rather use up scraps, self-striping or gradient yarns work beautifully here too — the continuous spiral construction means stripes form automatically without any color-change fuss.

These gloves also make an easy matching set with a couple of our other quick patterns: the same skein of yarn used here typically has enough left over for a twisted headband, and a slightly heavier weight in the same colorway pairs well with our single crochet beanie. Working all three from one dye lot avoids the slight color variation that can show up between different skeins purchased separately, which matters more than people expect when the pieces are worn together.

Sizing up or down

Add or remove 2 chains from the starting round to adjust the circumference, and add or remove rounds from the straight sections to adjust the length. A tighter fit (useful if the fabric feels loose once worn) usually means removing 2 to 4 chains from the starting round; a looser, more relaxed fit means adding the same amount.

For wrist length, the six rounds before the thumb gap and the four rounds after it can each be lengthened independently — add rounds before the gap for gloves that reach further up the forearm, or after it for more coverage over the back of the hand. Since the whole piece is worked in a continuous spiral with no shaping, any of these adjustments are safe to make without throwing off the rest of the pattern.

Caring for finished gloves

Hand-washing in cool water with a gentle detergent, then laying the gloves flat to dry, keeps single crochet fabric from stretching out of shape the way a tumble dryer can. Reshaping them slightly while damp — smoothing the tube back into an even circumference before they dry — helps them keep their fit over repeated wears.

Always check the yarn label before washing, since some wool blends are machine-washable on a delicate or wool cycle while others need to stay hand-wash only. Either way, avoid high heat: it's the most common way handmade gloves end up felted or shrunken.

Between wears, storing the gloves flat rather than balled up or hung on a hook helps them keep their shape too, since single crochet fabric can develop stretched-out spots wherever it's under tension for long periods. A drawer or a flat basket works better than a hook by the door, even though the hook is more convenient — a small trade-off that keeps the gloves fitting well for longer.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: losing track of the round start and ending up with a spiral seam that drifts noticeably off to one side.
Fix: always move the stitch marker up into the first stitch of each new round as you go — skipping this for even a couple of rounds makes it hard to tell where you are.
Mistake: chaining the thumb gap too tightly, which makes Round 9 awkward to work into and can pucker the fabric.
Fix: chain the 6 stitches for the gap slightly looser than your regular tension, and go slowly on the following round while working into those chain stitches.
Mistake: making two gloves with different starting chains or different numbers of rounds without noticing until both are finished.
Fix: write down any adjustments you make to the first glove — chain count, extra rounds, hook size — and copy them exactly for the second one.
Mistake: skipping a gauge swatch and ending up with gloves that are noticeably tighter or looser than expected.
Fix: work a small swatch first, especially if your yarn or hook differs from what's listed — single crochet tension varies more between crocheters than most other stitches.

Frequently asked questions

How do I size fingerless gloves for my hand?

Measure around the widest part of your hand excluding the thumb, and adjust the starting chain up or down in multiples of 2 to match, keeping in mind single crochet fabric stretches slightly once worn.

How much yarn do I need for fingerless gloves?

A pair of adult fingerless gloves uses roughly 100 to 130 yards of worsted-weight yarn total, comfortably within a single skein.

What is the difference between these gloves and the kids fingerless mitts pattern?

The construction is identical, just scaled up: this version uses a larger starting chain and more rounds to fit an adult hand and wrist length.

What yarn weight and fiber works best for fingerless gloves?

Worsted-weight is the easiest starting point since gauge and hook sizing are well documented, but a wool or wool-blend yarn adds warmth for winter wear, while cotton or a cotton blend is more breathable for gloves worn indoors while typing or knitting.

Can I add a fold-over cuff or thumbhole ribbing to this pattern?

Yes — working a few extra rounds in single crochet through the back loop only at the cuff edge creates a ribbed look that folds over neatly, without changing the rest of the construction.

How do I wash and care for finished fingerless gloves?

Hand-wash in cool water with a gentle detergent and lay flat to dry, which keeps the stitches from stretching out of shape; check the yarn label first, since some wool blends can be machine washed on a delicate cycle.
