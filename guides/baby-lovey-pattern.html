Free Crochet Baby Lovey Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A small comfort blanket with a soft amigurumi head, sized to be easy for a baby to hold.

Updated 2026 · US terms · Blanket about 10in / 25cm square

My sister asked me for "something simple" for her first baby shower gift, and I almost talked myself into a full baby blanket before realizing what actually gets used every single day isn't the biggest project in the nursery, it's the small one that goes everywhere. A lovey checked every box: quick enough to finish in a weekend, useful for years rather than months, and simple enough that it became the pattern I've since made more times than almost anything else on this site.

What I didn't expect was how many questions I'd get about it afterward — mostly from first-time crocheters wanting to make sure they were getting the safety details right before gifting one. This guide covers the pattern itself plus the practical questions that come up most: what yarn to use, how to gift one thoughtfully, and how to keep it safe and well cared for over time.

This lovey combines a small flat square, using the same technique as our dishcloth pattern, with a soft amigurumi head from our mini ball pattern sewn onto one corner. Two familiar techniques combined into one genuinely useful baby gift.

Baby safety note: loveys are generally intended for babies with head and neck control (commonly from around 3 months), not newborns, and should be used according to current safe sleep guidance. Embroider the face with yarn rather than using plastic safety eyes for full child safety. See the FAQ below.

Materials

  • 1 skein soft, machine-washable worsted-weight yarn (about 80–100 yards)
  • 4.5mm (US size 7) crochet hook for the blanket square
  • 3.5mm hook for the amigurumi head
  • A small amount of polyester fiberfill stuffing
  • Embroidery thread, for a safe stitched face
  • Stitch marker, scissors, and a yarn needle

Gauge

14 sc and 16 rows measures about 4in / 10cm square with a 4.5mm hook and worsted yarn. Gauge isn't critical for the blanket square, though tighter tension on the head keeps the stuffing from showing.

Notes

  • Written in US terms. The blanket square uses the flat technique from our dishcloth pattern; the head uses the amigurumi technique from our mini ball pattern.
  • Use soft, machine-washable yarn labelled safe for babies.
  • Embroider the face rather than using safety eyes for a fully child-safe finish.

Pattern

Blanket square, Foundation

Chain 36.

Blanket square, Row 1

Single crochet in the 2nd chain from the hook, and in each chain across. Turn. (35 sts)

Blanket square, Rows 2–35

Chain 1, single crochet in each stitch across. Turn. (35 sts each row)

Repeat until the piece is square, roughly 35 rows for this gauge. Fasten off and weave in ends.

Head, Round 1

Using the 3.5mm hook, make a magic ring, 6 sc into ring, place marker. (6 sts)

Head, Round 2

Inc in every st around. (12 sts)

Head, Round 3

[sc 1, inc] around. (18 sts)

Head, Rounds 4–7

Sc in every st around, no shaping. (18 sts each round)

Head, Round 8

[sc 1, dec] around. (12 sts)

Begin stuffing firmly.

Head, Round 9

Dec in every st around, adding final stuffing. (6 sts)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing.

Assembly

Embroider a simple face onto the head with embroidery thread. Sew the head securely onto the centre of one edge of the blanket square, using small, tight stitches through several stitches on both pieces.

Choosing safe yarn

Yarn choice matters more for a baby item than almost any other project, since a lovey ends up mouthed, chewed, and washed constantly. A soft, machine-washable acrylic or cotton yarn labeled safe for babies is the easiest starting point — avoid novelty yarns with loose fibers, sequins, or anything that could shed small pieces, and skip mohair or other fuzzy yarns that can irritate delicate skin or shed fibers a baby might inhale.

Color and dye matter too, though less urgently: a light or pastel shade in a reputable, widely available yarn brand is a safe default if you're unsure about a specific dye lot. If the yarn is a gift for a baby with a family history of skin sensitivity, a quick wash of the finished item before gifting removes any manufacturing residue and is generally a good habit regardless.

Gifting a lovey

A lovey makes a lovely baby shower gift, but it's worth pairing it with a short, friendly note about age recommendations rather than assuming the parents already know. New parents get a lot of gifts fast, and a small card mentioning "best for around 3 months and up, once baby has head and neck control" takes the guesswork out of when to introduce it.

If you're making one for a specific baby who's already a few months old, ask the parents about any known sensitivities before choosing yarn, and consider making two or three in different simple colors so the family always has a spare on hand for laundry day — a genuinely appreciated practical touch that a single lovey doesn't offer.

It's worth resisting the urge to add extra flourishes that seem cute but add risk: buttons, ribbons, tiny bows, or anything glued or tied on rather than securely crocheted or sewn all introduce small parts that don't belong on an item meant for a baby to mouth and handle unsupervised. Keeping the design as simple as the original pattern — a plain embroidered face, a soft square, nothing extra — is part of what makes a handmade lovey a genuinely safe gift rather than just a cute one.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: attaching the head with just a few anchor stitches, which can loosen with the frequent handling a lovey gets.
Fix: sew the head on with several passes of yarn through multiple stitches on both pieces, and recheck the attachment periodically once the lovey is in use.
Mistake: using plastic safety eyes or other small attached pieces instead of an embroidered face.
Fix: embroider the face with yarn or embroidery thread instead — it's just as expressive and removes a potential small-parts risk entirely.
Mistake: under-stuffing the head, which leaves it floppy and prone to losing shape after repeated washing.
Fix: stuff firmly and evenly, working the fiberfill into the sides as you go rather than just filling the center, and add a little extra just before closing the final round.
Mistake: using a yarn that isn't machine-washable, which makes ongoing care much harder for a busy family.
Fix: check the yarn label before starting and choose a machine-washable acrylic or cotton blend — a lovey gets washed often, and hand-wash-only yarn adds real friction for the parents.

Frequently asked questions

What is a baby lovey?

A lovey is a small comfort item combining a soft square of blanket fabric with a small stuffed head or animal shape attached, sized to be easy for a baby to hold and carry.

Is a crochet lovey safe for a newborn?

Loveys and other soft toys are generally recommended for babies old enough to have some head and neck control, typically from around 3 months, rather than newborns, and should always follow current safe sleep guidance from a pediatrician.

How do I attach the head to the blanket square on a lovey?

Sew the head securely to the centre of one edge of the blanket square using the tail left from finishing the head, going through several stitches on both pieces rather than just a few anchor points.

Is it OK to give a handmade lovey as a baby shower gift?

Yes, with a short note about age recommendations included, since the parents will want to know it's intended for a slightly older baby with head and neck control rather than a newborn, and should check current safe sleep guidance before use.

How do I wash a crochet baby lovey?

Hand wash or use a gentle cycle in a mesh laundry bag with mild, fragrance-free detergent, then lay flat to dry. Check the yarn label to confirm it's machine-washable before choosing a cycle.

How often should I check a lovey for wear once a baby is using it?

It's worth a quick check every few weeks, and any time it comes out of the wash, for loose stitches, a loosening head attachment, or fraying yarn, since these are the areas most likely to wear with regular handling and chewing.
