Single Crochet vs Double Crochet: What's the Difference?

Pin this pattern

Two of the most common crochet stitches, and how to tell which one a project actually needs.

Updated 2026 · US terms · 9 min read

I remember being handed a pattern that just said "dc" with total confidence that everyone already knew what that meant, and staring at my hook trying to reverse-engineer it from a stitch diagram. Nobody sat me down and explained that single and double crochet aren't just "a small one and a big one" — they behave completely differently in a finished piece, and picking the wrong one for a project is why some people's first blanket comes out stiff as cardboard, or their first amigurumi has visible holes.

Single crochet (sc) and double crochet (dc) are the two stitches most patterns are built around, and beginners often learn them back to back without a clear sense of when to use which. This guide breaks down both, side by side.

Quick answer: Single crochet is short, dense, and worked with one yarn-over — it makes tight, sturdy fabric good for amigurumi and dishcloths. Double crochet is taller, worked with two yarn-overs, and makes looser, drapier, faster-growing fabric good for blankets and garments.

How to work single crochet

Steps

  1. Insert your hook into the next stitch.
  2. Yarn over and pull up a loop (2 loops now on the hook).
  3. Yarn over again and pull through both loops on the hook (1 loop remains — stitch complete).

How to work double crochet

Steps

  1. Yarn over before inserting your hook — this extra wrap is what makes the stitch taller.
  2. Insert your hook into the next stitch, yarn over, and pull up a loop (3 loops now on the hook).
  3. Yarn over and pull through the first 2 loops only (2 loops remain).
  4. Yarn over again and pull through the remaining 2 loops (1 loop remains — stitch complete).

Side-by-side comparison

  • Height: double crochet stands roughly twice as tall as single crochet.
  • Density: single crochet makes a dense, gap-free fabric; double crochet leaves small visible gaps between stitches.
  • Speed: double crochet grows a project roughly twice as fast, row for row.
  • Best for: single crochet suits amigurumi, dishcloths, and tight bags; double crochet suits blankets, garments, and quick makes.
  • Turning chain: single crochet uses a chain 1 that doesn't count as a stitch; double crochet's chain 3 does count as the first stitch of the row.

Where half double crochet fits in

There's a third stitch worth knowing about once single and double both feel comfortable: half double crochet (hdc). It's worked like double crochet's first two steps — yarn over, insert the hook, yarn over and pull up a loop — but then finishes in one motion by pulling through all three loops on the hook at once, instead of two separate steps.

The result sits genuinely in between: taller and faster than single crochet, denser and less holey than double crochet. It's a common choice for scarves and garments that want some drape without the more open, lacy look full double crochet produces — our half double crochet scarf pattern is built entirely around it.

Which should you learn first?

Most crocheters learn single crochet first, since its slower growth makes tension easier to control while you're still getting comfortable with the hook. Our basic crochet stitches guide and easiest first projects both start there before introducing double crochet.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: the row comes out with one stitch too many or too few.
Fix: this almost always comes down to the turning chain. In double crochet, the chain 3 counts as the first stitch of the row, so the first "real" stitch goes into the second stitch, not the first — and the last stitch of the row goes into the top of the previous row's turning chain, which is easy to skip entirely.
Mistake: double crochet fabric looks noticeably more holey than the pattern photo.
Fix: some gap between double crochet stitches is normal, but a very loose yarn-over going into each stitch exaggerates it. Try dropping to a hook one size smaller, which tightens the fabric without changing the stitch pattern.
Mistake: switching between single and double crochet leaves a stiff, uneven-looking seam.
Fix: this is usually a tension mismatch rather than a stitch-count error. Since double crochet naturally sits looser than single crochet, deliberately loosening your tension slightly on the row just before or after a switch helps the transition blend rather than pucker.

Frequently asked questions

Is double crochet the same as UK treble crochet?

Yes — UK stitch names shift up one level from US terms (UK dc = US sc, UK tr = US dc), which is why "US terms" matters. See our abbreviations glossary for the full comparison.

Which stitch uses more yarn?

Double crochet grows faster per stitch, but single crochet fabric is denser, so total yarn used for a given finished size ends up fairly similar — density matters more than stitch height.

Can I mix single and double crochet in the same project?

Yes, this is common and gives texture — granny squares, for example, alternate double crochet groups with chain spaces.

Where does half double crochet fit between the two?

Half double crochet sits between single and double in both height and density, using a yarn-over-first step like double crochet but pulling through all three loops at once instead of two separate steps. It's a good middle ground for projects like scarves that want more drape than single crochet without the more open look of double crochet.

Why does my double crochet fabric look holey compared to the pattern photo?

Some visible gaps between double crochet stitches are normal and expected, not a mistake. If the holes look larger than the photo, check your yarn-over tension going into each stitch, and try a hook one size smaller, which tightens the fabric without changing the stitch itself.
