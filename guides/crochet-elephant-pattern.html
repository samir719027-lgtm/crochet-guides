Free Crochet Elephant Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A soft, rounded elephant worked in single crochet from a head, body, trunk, two ears, and four legs — a gentle first amigurumi with plenty of room for character.

Updated 2026 · US terms · About 7in / 18cm tall

The first elephant I ever helped a class crochet ended up with a trunk that pointed straight up at the ceiling, like a tiny grey snorkel. The maker, a retired nurse named Joan, looked at it, laughed, and announced that her grandson was getting an elephant called Snorkel whether she liked it or not. I've made peace with that. When you crochet an elephant, the trunk and the ears are where all the personality hides, and a slightly crooked trunk reads as character rather than a mistake.

That's part of why I love this pattern for beginners. An elephant is just a handful of soft shapes joined together — a round head, a rounder body, four little legs, two flat ears, and one tapering trunk — and every single one of them is built from single crochet in the round. If you can work a magic ring and count an increase, you can make this elephant. There's no fiddly shaping to wrestle with, and the finished toy has enough charm to hold its own against the fanciest shop-bought softies.

This guide gives you the full pattern in US terms, with stitch counts for every round so you can check yourself as you go. I've also included the mistakes I see most often in class and exactly how to fix them, so you don't have to find out the hard way. Ready? Let's pick a yarn and start.

Materials

  • 1–2 skeins worsted-weight yarn in your main colour (about 90–110 yards)
  • A small amount of contrast yarn for the inner ears
  • 3.5mm hook (smaller than standard, for tight amigurumi stitches)
  • Polyester fiberfill stuffing
  • Safety eyes, or embroidery thread for a child-safe embroidered face
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors and a yarn needle

Gauge

Gauge isn't critical — the goal is tight, even stitches that hide the stuffing. If you can see stuffing through your stitches, try a smaller hook.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral, the same technique as our mini amigurumi ball pattern.
  • Made from nine separate pieces (head, body, trunk, 2 ears, 4 legs) sewn together at the end.
  • If the magic ring still feels fiddly, start with our step-by-step magic ring tutorial.
  • For a child under 3, skip plastic safety eyes and embroider the face instead.

Pattern

Head, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Head, Rounds 5–8

Sc every st around for 4 rounds, no shaping. (24 sts each round)

Head, Rounds 9–11

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the body.

Body, Rounds 1–5

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24) → [sc 3, inc] around. (30)

Body, Rounds 6–9

Sc every st around for 4 rounds. (30 sts each round)

Body, Rounds 10–13

[sc 3, dec] around. (24) → [sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the head.

Trunk

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping, stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing.

Ears (make 2)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18). Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing on flat, without stuffing.

Legs (make 4)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping, stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing.

Assembly

Sew the two ears flat onto the sides of the head, low and even. Sew the trunk to the front centre of the head just below the eyes, curving it gently. Sew the body to the bottom of the head, then the four legs evenly around the base of the body. Attach safety eyes before stuffing the head if using them, or embroider a simple face with yarn once assembly is complete for a child-safe alternative.

Choosing yarn & colour

I recommend worsted-weight yarn for a first elephant because you can see your stitches clearly, and a 3.5mm hook gives you the tight fabric that hides stuffing. A soft acrylic is my usual go-to for toys that get hugged and dropped and washed, because it holds its shape and doesn't shed the way some natural fibres can. If you want a firmer, more sculpted elephant, a mercerised cotton is lovely, though it takes a little more effort on your hands. Our guide to the best yarn for amigurumi walks through the trade-offs if you're torn.

Grey is the classic elephant, but nothing about this pattern demands it. I've taught this in soft blue, dusty pink, warm mustard, and a very serious navy that one student insisted made her elephant look like it worked in banking. The only colour choice I'd steer you toward is a contrast inner ear — even a scrap of cream or blush makes the finished elephant look intentional rather than just grey-on-grey. If you're not confident with colour changes yet, you can simply sew a smaller circle of contrast fabric or felt on top of each crocheted ear instead, and no one will ever know.

Getting the sizing right

With worsted-weight yarn and a 3.5mm hook, this elephant comes out around 7 inches tall, which sits nicely in a small hand or on a nursery shelf. If you use a DK or sport-weight yarn with a smaller hook, you'll get a smaller, tighter elephant; with bulky yarn and a bigger hook, it grows quickly. The shaping is the same, so don't overthink it — just keep your tension consistent across every piece so the head and body match.

If you'd rather scale the written pattern, the rules are simple. To go bigger, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds, and one more decrease round at the end. To go smaller, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two. Keep the proportions even: whatever you add to the head, add the same to the body, otherwise you'll end up with a bobble-headed elephant that tips forward.

Sewing the trunk & ears

The ears come first, because they do the most to make the head read as an elephant. Sew each ear on flat — not stuffed — and position it low on the side of the head, roughly level with the eyes rather than up on top. Pin or hold it in place and look at the head straight on before you commit, because an ear an eighth of an inch higher than the other is the first thing anyone notices. A whipstitch around the whole edge holds far better than three or four anchor stitches and stops the ear from cupping inward.

The trunk is the piece people worry about most, and it's also the most forgiving. It's just a short tube, worked flat from a six-stitch magic ring, and it curls exactly the way you shape it. Stuff it lightly and evenly, curve it gently with your fingers, and sew it to the front centre of the head just below the eyes. If you want a particular curl to stay put, run one stitch through the base of the trunk and back into the head; that little anchor is invisible and makes all the difference.

Last, sew the body to the bottom of the head with small, tight stitches, and add a little extra stuffing through the last gap before you close it fully. Space the four legs evenly around the base of the body so the elephant sits squarely instead of rocking. If the whole assembly step is new to you, our amigurumi for beginners guide covers attaching pieces in detail.

Gifting & finishing touches

This elephant is one of the most gifted things I know how to make. It's my go-to for baby showers and new arrivals, and I keep a couple of half-made heads in my project bag so I can finish one in an evening when a birthday sneaks up on me. If the elephant is for a child under three, embroider the eyes and any face details with yarn instead of using safety eyes — it's the safest choice and, honestly, an embroidered smile has a warmth that plastic eyes can't match.

A small finishing touch makes a plain elephant feel finished. A ribbon bow at the neck, a few rows of single crochet worked as a tiny blanket, or a scrap-yarn chain tied around the trunk all take minutes. If you embroider a little crescent of sleeping eyes instead of open ones, you've got a baby elephant that reads instantly as a newborn gift. Before you wrap it, run your fingers over every seam and give each limb a gentle tug — if anything shifts, sew it again now rather than after the first wash.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: the ears end up sewn on at different heights or angles, so the face looks lopsided.
Fix: pin both ears on and check them straight on and from directly above before you stitch, and use the same row of stitches as a guide for both sides.
Mistake: the trunk comes out too fat, too thin, or pointing the wrong way.
Fix: count your six stitches in the first round and stuff lightly rather than tightly; a thin, overstuffed trunk can't curve naturally, so shape it with your fingers and anchor it with a stitch through the base.
Mistake: the elephant rocks or wobbles because the legs are uneven.
Fix: stuff all four legs to the same firmness, compare them side by side before sewing, and space them evenly around the base of the body so the weight sits square.
Mistake: you can see stuffing through the stitches and the fabric looks gappy.
Fix: go down a hook size so the stitches tighten up, and pull each stitch a little snug as you work; it's better to start again with a smaller hook than to finish a floppy, see-through elephant.
Mistake: safety eyes end up crooked or too far apart once inserted.
Fix: insert the eye posts but don't lock the backings on yet, hold the head up and check the spacing from a normal viewing distance, and only then snap the backings into place.
Mistake: the head wobbles on the body or tips forward.
Fix: sew around the whole join with small, tight stitches rather than a few anchor points, and add a little more stuffing through the last gap before you close it so the neck sits firm.

Frequently asked questions

Is this crochet elephant pattern safe for babies and toddlers?

For children under 3, embroider the face with yarn instead of using plastic safety eyes, and sew every seam closed with small, tight stitches. Even well-secured safety eyes carry a small-parts risk, so embroidering is the safest choice for the youngest children.

How long does the elephant take to make?

Most beginners finish the elephant over two or three sessions, roughly 3 to 4 hours of crochet time plus assembly. The head and body are the slowest parts; the legs, ears, and trunk work up quickly.

Do I have to crochet the trunk separately?

Yes. Working the trunk as its own small tube and sewing it on is the easiest way for a beginner to get a smooth, curved trunk that sits naturally, rather than trying to shape it directly off the head.

How do I stop the ears from flopping over?

Sew the ears on flat with a whipstitch around the whole edge, not just a few anchor points, and use the same tension you crocheted with. Position them low on the sides of the head, and they will sit naturally without drooping.

What's the best yarn for a soft elephant?

A soft worsted-weight acrylic or cotton works best. Acrylic is the classic choice for a toy that will be hugged and washed, because it stays soft, holds its shape, and does not shed the way some natural fibres can.

Why does my elephant's trunk curl the wrong way?

The trunk curls the way it was stuffed and sewn. Stuff it lightly and evenly, curve it gently with your fingers before you sew it on, and run a stitch through the base if you want it to hold a particular curve.

Can I make the elephant bigger or smaller?

Yes. For a bigger elephant, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds, and one more decrease round at the end. For a smaller one, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two.
