Free Crochet Bunny Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A soft sitting bunny with long ears, built on the same shaping as our mini ball pattern but with a rounder head, two feet, and a fluffy little tail.

Updated 2026 · US terms · About 7in / 18cm tall

I've taught this bunny in my classes for years, and the moment I always look forward to is when someone holds up their finished head and realises the two flat shapes they're about to crochet are going to become ears. One spring I ran a weekend workshop where a student came back on the Sunday with a bunny whose ears were exactly the same length but sewn on so differently that one flopped forward and the other stood straight up. Her four-year-old named it Good Ear, Bad Ear, and it became the most loved toy in their house. That's the honest truth about amigurumi: the rounds are simple, and the personality lives in the placement.

The mistake I see most often in my classes isn't a wrong stitch count — it's people pulling their magic ring so tight the first round puckers, or working so loosely that the stuffing shows through. If you can make a magic ring and a single crochet, you can make this bunny. Everything after that is patience and counting. I've written the whole thing to be worked in a continuous spiral, which means you never join rounds or turn; you just keep going and let a stitch marker do the remembering for you.

This bunny is a gentle step up from a plain ball. You'll make a head, a body, two long ears, two arms, two feet, and a round little tail, then sew them all together. None of it is hard, but the finished toy looks far more impressive than the sum of its parts — which, honestly, is the best trick a beginner can pull off on a first or second project.

Work the head first. It uses the exact same shaping as our mini amigurumi ball pattern, so by the time you've closed the top of the head you'll have practised every technique the rest of the bunny needs: increases, plain rounds, decreases, and sewing the hole shut.

Materials

  • 1–2 skeins worsted-weight yarn (about 90–120 yards)
  • 3.5mm hook (smaller than standard, for tight amigurumi stitches)
  • Polyester fiberfill stuffing
  • Safety eyes, or embroidery thread for a child-safe embroidered face
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors and a yarn needle

Abbreviations (US terms)

Abbr.Means
scSingle crochet
st(s)Stitch(es)
inc2 sc in same stitch (increase)
decSc 2 sts together (decrease)
rnd(s)Round(s)

Gauge

Gauge isn't critical — the goal is tight, even stitches that hide the stuffing. If you can see stuffing through your stitches, try a smaller hook.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral. If this is your first time, see our magic ring tutorial and our guide to crocheting in the round.
  • Made from eight separate pieces (head, body, 2 arms, 2 feet, 2 ears, 1 tail) sewn together at the end.
  • For a child under 3, skip plastic safety eyes and embroider the face instead.

Pattern

Head, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Head, Rounds 5–9

Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping. (24 sts each round)

Head, Rounds 10–12

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the body.

Body, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Body, Rounds 5–8

Sc every st around for 4 rounds. (24 sts each round)

Body, Rounds 9–11

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the head.

Arms (make 2)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping, stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing.

Feet (make 2)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Inc around. (12). Sc every st around for 2 rounds. (12). Then [sc 2, dec] around. (9). Sc every st around for 1 round. (9), stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing, and flatten the foot before attaching.

Ears (make 2)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). [sc 1, inc] around. (9). Sc every st around for 12 rounds, no shaping and no stuffing. (9 sts each round). Then [sc 1, dec] around. (6). Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing.

Tail (make 1)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Inc around. (12). Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing, and stuff lightly.

Assembly

Sew the body to the bottom of the head using the tails from each. Sew the two ears onto the top of the head, the two arms and two feet onto the body, and the tail onto the back, checking placement is even before securing each with small, tight stitches. Attach safety eyes before stuffing the head if using them, or embroider a simple face with yarn once assembly is complete for a child-safe alternative.

Choosing yarn & colours

Worsted-weight yarn is the easiest place to start: it's thick enough to hide the stuffing and shows your stitches clearly, so you can see what you're doing while you learn. A cotton or cotton-blend yarn keeps its stitch definition, which makes a bunny with crisp, even rounds; an acrylic or acrylic blend is softer, fluffier, and cheaper if you're making several at once. Both work here, so go with whichever feels better in your hands. If you'd like the longer answer on trade-offs, our guide to the best yarn for amigurumi goes through it in detail.

For colour, you genuinely cannot go wrong with a soft natural tone — cream, oat, warm grey, or a muted fawn brown all read as "bunny" before you've even added the face. If you want a bit more personality, a pale blush or butter-yellow body with white inner ears is a lovely combination. I usually suggest beginners keep the body one colour and save any contrast for the inside of the ears, the nose, and the tail. That way the single-colour rounds stay easy to count and you still get a finished toy that looks designed rather than improvised.

Sizing it up or down

The finished bunny stands about 7in / 18cm tall from the bottom of its feet to the tips of its ears, but the pattern scales cleanly. To make a bigger bunny, add one more increase round to both the head and body before the straight rounds — so [sc 3, inc] around would take you to 30 stitches — then work one extra plain round and one extra decrease round to match. A chunkier yarn with a 4mm or 4.5mm hook is the quickest way to grow the whole thing without changing a single number, and it's the route I recommend if you want a cuddly, bed-sized bunny.

For a smaller bunny, do the reverse: stop one increase round earlier on the head and body, and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two. If you'd rather keep the pattern identical but shrink the result, switch to a DK or sport-weight yarn and a 2.5mm or 3mm hook — the proportions stay exactly the same and you end up with a palm-sized bunny. Just remember that the smaller you go, the more important it is to keep your tension even, because any looseness shows up far more clearly at a small scale.

Embroidering the face

The face is where the bunny comes alive, and it's also the part I'd most strongly encourage beginners to embroider rather than skip. A little "V" or triangle nose worked in pink or brown yarn, with a single vertical stitch dropping down from its base and two small horizontal stitches for the mouth, gives a sweet, gentle expression in about five minutes. Two short straight stitches for closed sleeping eyes turn the same bunny into a completely different toy, and three fine whisker lines on each cheek add a lot of character for almost no effort.

Embroidering is also the child-safe option. If the bunny is for anyone under three, skip the safety eyes entirely and stitch the eyes on with yarn instead; plastic safety eyes, even when locked on tightly, carry a small-parts risk for babies and toddlers. Work the face after assembly is complete, using a length of the same or contrasting yarn and a yarn needle, and tie the ends off inside the head so nothing dangles. If you're new to it, start with the nose and eyes first, step back and look at the face from arm's length, and only add whiskers or brows once you're happy with the expression.

Gifting your bunny

A handmade bunny is one of the most satisfying gifts to give, partly because it's a soft toy that actually gets carried around and slept on. If it's for a baby shower or a first birthday, embroider the face rather than using safety eyes, and mention to the parents that it should be spot-cleaned and checked now and then for loose threads. If it's for an older child or an adult who'll appreciate the effort, a tiny scrap-yarn scarf or a ribbon bow tied at one ear takes minutes and makes the whole thing feel finished.

When I give one of these away, I always include a little card with the washing advice and a one-line note about which yarn I used, in case it ever needs a repair in the same colour. It's a small touch, but the parents in my classes tell me it's the difference between a toy that gets displayed on a shelf and one that gets loved to bits and mended rather than thrown away. Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: the ears end up different lengths or flop in different directions, so the bunny looks uneven.
Fix: lay both ears side by side and count the rounds before sewing anything on, then pin each one in place and check the face head-on from arm's length before you commit to stitching.
Mistake: the safety eyes look too far apart or slightly crossed once they're in.
Fix: insert the eye posts but don't lock the backings on yet — hold the head up and check the spacing from a normal viewing distance before securing them permanently, and remember the nose sits between and just below them.
Mistake: you can see stuffing poking through the stitches, especially around the decreases.
Fix: go down a hook size so the stitches sit tighter, and use smaller pieces of fiberfill pushed in a little at a time rather than one big handful.
Mistake: one ear curls or twists to the side so the two ears point in different directions.
Fix: continuous spirals naturally lean a little, and narrow pieces exaggerate it. When you sew the ear on, rotate it so the lean faces backward where it's barely noticeable, and keep your tension firm and even while working the ear.
Mistake: the head looks lumpy or bigger on one side once it's stuffed.
Fix: stuff the head in small, even pieces rather than one large ball, and keep rolling it in your hands as you go so the filling settles evenly before you close the hole.
Mistake: the magic ring puckers and the first round won't lie flat.
Fix: pull the tail just enough to close the centre, not until the stitches bunch, then use the tail to tighten a fraction more after the second round is done.

Frequently asked questions

Is this crochet bunny pattern safe for young children?

For children under 3, embroider the facial features with yarn instead of using plastic safety eyes, since even securely attached safety eyes carry some small-parts risk for very young children.

How long does this crochet bunny take to make?

Including the head, body, ears, arms, and feet, most beginners finish this bunny over two to three sessions, roughly 3 to 4 hours of total crochet time plus assembly.

Can I make the bunny bigger or smaller?

Yes. To scale it up, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds, and one more decrease round at the end. For a smaller bunny, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two.

How do I get the ears to stand up?

Sew each ear on with a wide base of small, tight stitches and keep your tension firm. If you want them to stand straighter, add a couple of extra plain rounds near the base of each ear or stuff just the bottom half of the ear very lightly.

What's the best stuffing for a crochet bunny?

Polyester fiberfill is the standard choice because it stays soft, holds its shape, and is washable and hypoallergenic, unlike natural fibers that can clump or felt over time.

Why does my bunny's ear curl or twist?

Continuous spirals naturally lean slightly, and narrow pieces like ears exaggerate that lean into a visible curl. Work with firm, even tension, and when you sew the ear on, rotate it so the lean faces backward where it is barely noticeable.

Can I wash a finished crochet bunny?

Spot clean with a damp cloth and mild soap where possible; a full wash risks matting the stuffing and loosening safety eyes, so it's best avoided unless the bunny is machine-washable by design.
