10 Cozy Crochet Blanket Patterns You Need to Try

Pin this pattern

From a super bulky throw you can finish in a weekend to a year-long temperature blanket — ten blanket patterns worth adding to your queue.

Updated 2026 · 8 min read

I get asked some version of "what blanket should I make next?" more than almost any other question, and for a long time I didn't have a good answer beyond "whatever you're in the mood for." That's a fine answer, but it's not a very useful one if you're staring at a queue of a hundred saved patterns and can't decide where to start. So this is the list I wish I'd had: ten blankets from across this site, chosen specifically because each one teaches or showcases something a little different, from a first-ever texture stitch to a genuinely long-term project.

I didn't pick these because they're the fastest or the easiest; a couple of them, like the temperature blanket, are deliberately slow projects you live with for a year. What they have in common is that each one earns its place on a list like this: a distinct look, a specific skill or technique at its center, and a finished size that makes sense for how it's meant to be used, whether that's a nursery, a car, or your own sofa.

Below, each pattern links straight to its full free instructions on this site, with a short note on what makes it worth making and who it's best suited for. If you're new to blankets specifically, our home & décor category has the full collection, including a few smaller projects to warm up on first.

How to pick the right one

If you're choosing based on how much time you have, start from the ends of this list rather than the middle: the chunky throw and the lapghan are both designed to move fast, while the temperature blanket and the mermaid tail are the two genuine time investments here, one because it spans a year and the other because the scale stitch takes patience. Everything else sits comfortably in between, at roughly the pace of a normal multi-week blanket project.

If you're choosing based on who it's for, the C2C and easy baby blankets are the obvious picks for a baby shower, the lapghan makes a thoughtful gift for someone in a wheelchair or recliner, and the granny square blanket is the safest general-purpose gift, since almost everyone has room in their life for a soft, colorful throw.

It's also worth thinking about where the blanket will actually live once it's finished. A blanket meant for a busy sofa or a kid's bed benefits from a sturdier, more densely worked stitch like the bobble or diamond trellis, since those textures hide everyday wear better than a very open, airy pattern would. A blanket meant purely for display, like the mermaid tail, can afford to prioritize visual drama over practicality, since it's less likely to be balled up and dragged around daily.

Yarn tips across these patterns

Worsted-weight yarn covers most of this list, with the chunky throw as the one clear exception, since it specifically calls for super bulky yarn and a larger hook to get that fast-growing, nubby texture. If you're substituting a yarn weight in any of these patterns, check the individual pattern's gauge section first, since swapping weights changes both your final size and how much yarn you'll need; our guide to estimating yarn walks through the math if you're not sure where to start.

For anything going to a baby or living in a car, like the C2C, easy baby, and lapghan patterns, stick to a machine-washable acrylic or acrylic blend rather than a natural fiber that needs special care; it's a small choice up front that saves a lot of hassle later.

Buying yarn for a multi-skein blanket also benefits from checking dye lots before you start, especially for patterns like the temperature blanket where you'll be returning to the same colors across many months. A single dye-lot mismatch is easy to miss in a small project but can leave a visible seam of slightly different shading across a large, single-color background, so it's worth buying a little extra of your main colors up front rather than risking a return trip mid-project.

Frequently asked questions

Which crochet blanket pattern is best for a total beginner?

The chunky crochet throw and the easy baby blanket are the most forgiving starting points, since both use a single stitch repeated over and over with no color changes or stitch counting to track. The granny square blanket is also very beginner-friendly once you can make one square, since the rest of the project is just repetition.

How much yarn do I need for a full-size blanket?

Most throw-sized blankets in this roundup use somewhere between 800 and 1,800 yards of worsted-weight yarn depending on the stitch and finished size, with bulkier stitches like the chunky throw using less yardage but more bulk. Check the materials section of each individual pattern for its specific amount, and see our guide to estimating yarn if you're substituting a different weight.

What's the fastest blanket to make from this list?

The chunky throw and the lapghan are both designed to work up quickly, the chunky throw because super bulky yarn and a large hook cover ground fast, and the lapghan because its small, car-blanket size means far fewer rows than a full throw.

Which blanket pattern makes the best gift?

The C2C and easy baby blankets are popular baby shower gifts, the temperature blanket makes a genuinely personal gift tied to a specific year or milestone, and the lapghan is a thoughtful, quick-to-finish option for anyone in a wheelchair or recliner who would find a full-size throw too bulky.

Can I mix and match stitches from different patterns in this list?

Yes, within reason. Because most of these patterns are built from the same basic single or double crochet base with one distinguishing texture or color technique, it's fairly straightforward to borrow, say, the bobble accent row from the lapghan and add it to a plain throw, as long as you keep your stitch counts consistent.

How do I know what size to make my blanket?

Each pattern lists its own finished size, but as a general guide, a lapghan or car blanket is usually around 30 by 40 inches, a baby blanket is typically 30 to 36 inches square, and a full throw blanket is commonly 50 inches or larger in each direction.
