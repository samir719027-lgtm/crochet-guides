Crochet Granny Square: Complete Beginner Tutorial

Pin this pattern

The exact stitches for your first granny square, written in US terms, from magic ring to finished square.

Updated 2026 · 11 min read

My grandmother taught me my first granny square at her kitchen table, using a hook that was older than I was and a bag of leftover acrylic in colours that didn't remotely match. I got through Round 1 without trouble, hit Round 2, and produced something that looked less like a square and more like a slightly deflated kite. She didn't tell me what I'd done wrong right away — she just handed me a seam ripper and said "again." I've since made more granny squares than I could count, and I still remember Round 2 as the moment the pattern actually clicked, because it's the first round where you have to read your stitches instead of just following a count.

The granny square earns its reputation as the classic first "real" crochet project for good reason: it only uses chain and double crochet, it works in rounds with no shaping to worry about, and it teaches you colour-changing and corner construction in one small, forgiving piece. This tutorial covers the same three rounds my grandmother taught me, written the way I wish someone had written them down for me at the time — with the specific moments people get stuck spelled out, not just the stitch counts.

Written in US terms. If you're not sure what that means, our abbreviations glossary explains the difference between US and UK stitch names before you start — it trips up more beginners than the stitches themselves.

What you need

  • A 5.0mm hook (see our hook buying guide if you don't have one). For your very first square, a slightly bigger hook than the yarn label suggests makes the stitches easier to see and count.
  • Worsted-weight yarn in one or more colours. Smooth cotton or acrylic is easier to see your stitches in than fuzzy or dark yarn — save the mohair for later.
  • Scissors and a yarn needle for weaving in ends.
  • A stitch marker or a spare bit of yarn, to mark your starting stitch each round. This one habit prevents most of the "wonky square" problems in the mistakes section below.

How a granny square is actually built

Before the steps, it helps to know what you're building toward, because a granny square is really the same move repeated with one small addition each round: a corner group (3 double crochet, chain 2, 3 double crochet, all in the same space) at each of the four corners, and a side group (3 double crochet) in between. Round 1 has no side groups yet — just four corners meeting each other directly. From Round 2 onward, every round adds one more side group per edge than the round before it. Once that clicks, you stop needing to count rows and start recognising the shape as you go, which is the actual skill, not the stitch count.

Round 1: the centre

Steps

  1. Chain 4, then join into a ring with a slip stitch into the first chain.
  2. Chain 3 (this counts as your first double crochet).
  3. Work 2 more double crochet into the ring.
  4. Chain 2 (this forms the corner).
  5. Work [3 double crochet, chain 2] into the ring three more times — you'll have 4 groups of 3 double crochet, each separated by a chain-2 corner.
  6. Join with a slip stitch to the top of your starting chain-3. Fasten off if changing colour, or continue to Round 2.

Everything in Round 1 goes into the same small ring, so it will look crowded and slightly bunched — that's expected, not a mistake. It opens out once Round 2 gives it somewhere to go.

Round 2: building the square

Steps

  1. Join your yarn (new colour or same) into any chain-2 corner space.
  2. Chain 3, then work [2 double crochet, chain 2, 3 double crochet] into that same corner space — this makes the corner.
  3. Chain 1, then work 3 double crochet into the next chain-2 space (this is now a side, not a corner).
  4. Repeat: corner in each corner space, chain 1 and 3 double crochet in each side space, going around all 4 sides.
  5. Join with a slip stitch to the top of your starting chain-3.
Where people get stuck. This is the round my square went sideways on, more than once. The mistake is almost always working into the gap between stitch groups instead of into the chain-space itself. Every "3 double crochet" and every corner goes into the open chain space (the little hole made by the chain-1 or chain-2), never into the top of a stitch. If you can't find the space, spread the previous round flat on the table and look for the gaps — they're more obvious than they feel with yarn in your hands.

Round 3: repeat the pattern

Steps

Round 3 repeats the same logic as Round 2, just with one more side section per edge:

  1. Join into any corner space, chain 3, then [2 double crochet, chain 2, 3 double crochet] in that corner.
  2. Chain 1, 3 double crochet in the next space, chain 1, 3 double crochet in the next space (two side groups now, instead of one).
  3. Repeat around all 4 sides, then join with a slip stitch to close the round.

Every additional round follows this same pattern: a corner group in each corner, and one more side group per edge than the round before it. Once you've done Round 3, Round 8 is not actually harder — it's just more of the same decision, repeated.

Common mistakes & fixes

Square is cupping or curling instead of lying flat. This means your tension is too tight, which pulls the corners in faster than the sides can spread. Go up half a hook size, and deliberately loosen your tension specifically on the chain-2 corners — those are what let the square grow outward.
Square isn't actually square — one side looks longer. This is almost always a miscounted corner somewhere in an earlier round. Each corner needs exactly 3 double crochet, chain 2, 3 double crochet in the same space; one extra or missing stitch there throws off that whole side going forward. Count your corners before you count your sides.
Lost track of where the round started. This is what the stitch marker in your materials list is for. Drop it into your starting stitch at the beginning of every round and you'll never have to guess where to join.

Finishing and joining squares

Fasten off and weave in your ends with a yarn needle. For a single coaster, you're done. To make a blanket, crochet multiple squares to the same round count, then join them either by whip stitching the edges together with a yarn needle, or by single crocheting them together as a border — whip stitching is the simpler option for a first attempt, and it's the one I still reach for first.

Frequently asked questions

How many rounds does a basic granny square have?

A basic granny square is often worked to 3 or 4 rounds for a coaster-sized square, roughly 4 to 5 inches across. You can keep adding rounds to make it as large as you like for a blanket.

Do I need to change yarn colour for each round of a granny square?

No, colour changes are a style choice, not a requirement. A granny square made in a single colour throughout is just as structurally correct as a multicoloured one.

How do I join granny squares together into a blanket?

The two most common methods are whip stitching the edges together with a yarn needle, or single crocheting the squares together as a border. Whip stitching is simpler for a first attempt; single crochet joining gives a neater, more finished edge.

Why is my granny square curling or cupping instead of lying flat?

Cupping almost always means your stitches are too tight, which pulls the corners in faster than the sides can spread. Try going up half a hook size, and consciously loosen your tension on the chain-2 corners specifically, since those are what let the square grow outward.

Why does my granny square look wonky or not actually square?

This is almost always a miscounted corner. Each corner needs exactly 3 double crochet, chain 2, 3 double crochet, worked into the same space. If one corner has an extra or missing stitch, that side will grow at a different rate and pull the whole square off true.
