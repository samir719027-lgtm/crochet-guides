Free Crochet Unicorn Pattern for Beginners

Pin this pattern

A soft amigurumi unicorn made from simple single-crochet balls, with a tapering yarn horn and a looped yarn mane that hides any wobbly seaming.

Updated 2026 · US terms · About 7in / 18cm tall

When I teach amigurumi, I always save the unicorn for the second or third project, because the moment someone understands a magic ring and a single crochet increase, they can make one and feel like they've cracked the whole craft. My first unicorn, made years ago, had a horn I stitched on at a forty-five-degree angle because I was in too much of a hurry to check it before tying off. It leaned so far forward that my niece called it “the polite unicorn,” and it sat on her shelf for a decade anyway. That's the thing about this pattern: the rounds are gentle, and the mistakes that actually happen are fixable, usually with nothing more than a yarn needle and a bit of patience.

Everything here is built from a single crochet worked in a continuous spiral, in US terms. The head and body are simple balls, the horn tapers from a four-stitch magic ring, and the ears are tiny flat discs. None of it is difficult, but each piece teaches you a real amigurumi skill: increases, decreases, working small, and sewing pieces together so they sit straight. If you've already finished our mini amigurumi ball pattern, you know most of what's coming.

The mane and horn are made entirely from yarn, which matters if this unicorn is for a baby or toddler. There are no plastic horns to glue, no button eyes to sew on for the under-threes, and nothing small enough to pull off and swallow. I'll walk through the mane in the technique section below, but the short version is this: a bundle of cut strands, looped through the back of the head, trimmed to length. Ten minutes of work, and it hides any wobble in your seaming.

Good to know: the mane is deliberately forgiving. It sits along the back of the head and neck, right where your seaming between head and body will be, so if your join is a little uneven, the yarn strands cover it. This is the one pattern where I tell beginners not to stress about a perfect seam.

Materials

  • 1 skein worsted-weight yarn in your main pastel colour (about 80–100 yards)
  • Scrap yarn in a contrasting colour for the horn, ears, and mane
  • 3.5mm hook (smaller than standard, for tight amigurumi stitches)
  • Polyester fiberfill stuffing
  • Safety eyes, or embroidery thread for a child-safe embroidered face
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors and a yarn needle

Gauge

Gauge isn't critical — the goal is tight, even stitches that hide the stuffing. If you can see stuffing through your stitches, try a smaller hook.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral, same technique as our mini amigurumi ball pattern.
  • Made from nine separate pieces (head, body, 4 legs, horn, 2 ears) plus the yarn mane, sewn together at the end.
  • The mane and horn are made from yarn, so there are no small detachable parts — safe for babies when you embroider the face.
  • New to the starting technique? Read our magic ring tutorial first if the first round feels fiddly.

Pattern

Head, Rounds 1–4

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18) → [sc 2, inc] around. (24)

Head, Rounds 5–9

Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping. (24 sts each round)

Head, Rounds 10–12

[sc 2, dec] around. (18) → [sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing firmly as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the body.

Body, Rounds 1–3

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6) → Inc around. (12) → [sc 1, inc] around. (18)

Body, Rounds 4–8

Sc every st around for 5 rounds. (18 sts each round)

Body, Rounds 9–10

[sc 1, dec] around. (12) → Dec around, stuffing as you close. (6)

Fasten off, leaving a long tail for sewing to the head.

Legs (make 4)

Magic ring, 6 sc. (6). Sc every st around for 5 rounds, no shaping, stuffing lightly. Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing.

Horn

Magic ring, 4 sc. (4). Sc every st around. (4). [sc 1, inc] around. (6). Sc every st around. (6). Fasten off, leaving a tail for sewing.

Ears (make 2)

Magic ring, 5 sc into ring. Fasten off, leaving a short tail for sewing on flat, without stuffing.

Mane

Cut strands of yarn about 30cm long. Fold each in half and use a latch-hook or yarn needle to pull the looped fold through a stitch along the back of the head, drawing the cut ends through the loop to secure it. Work from between the ears down the back of the neck, then trim to the length you like.

Assembly

Sew the body to the bottom of the head using the tails from each. Sew the four legs onto the body, the horn to the centre of the top of the head, and the two ears either side of it, checking placement is even before securing each with small, tight stitches. Attach safety eyes before stuffing the head if using them, or embroider a simple face with yarn once assembly is complete for a child-safe alternative.

Choosing pastel colours

Unicorns suit pastels so well that the colour choice does a lot of the design work for you. I steer beginners toward a light, solid shade for the body — soft pink, pale lilac, mint, or a very pale blue — because a smooth solid colour shows your stitches clearly, so you can spot a missed increase or a dropped stitch as it happens rather than three rounds later. A variegated or dark yarn looks lovely, but it hides the stitch definition you need while you're still learning to read your own work.

The horn and mane are where you introduce a second and third colour without any complicated colour changes in the round, because they're attached separately. A gold or cream horn against a pink body reads instantly as “unicorn,” and the mane can be as many shades as you like, since it's just cut strands. One of my students made an all-white unicorn with a rainbow mane of leftover scraps, and it was the most-loved thing on her stall at the school fair.

If you're unsure which yarn to pick before you start, our guide to the best yarn for amigurumi walks through the trade-offs between fibres, and why a worsted-weight acrylic tends to be the friendliest place to begin.

The mane & horn technique

The horn is the one place where tension genuinely matters. You start with a four-stitch magic ring and work four stitches, then six, which gives you a cone that's narrow at the tip and wider at the base. Because it's so small, even a slightly loose stitch shows up as a bump in the surface, so keep those rounds snug and stuff the horn firmly before you sew it on. A limp, under-stuffed horn flops forward, and that's where my “polite unicorn” came from.

The mane is easier and, honestly, more fun. Cut your mane yarn into strands of roughly 30cm, fold each strand in half, and use a latch-hook or the eye of a yarn needle to pull the looped fold through a stitch along the back of the head. Then draw the two cut ends through that loop and pull gently — you've just made a secure knot that won't come loose the way a sewn-on wig would. Work a row from between the ears down the back of the neck, keeping the strands close together so the mane looks full, and trim the ends to whatever length suits the size of your unicorn.

The best part is that the mane covers the join between head and body, which is the seam most beginners worry about. Put your energy into the face and let the mane hide the rest. If you want a longer, more layered mane, add a second row just below the first and let the top layer fall over it.

Sizing & proportion

As written, this unicorn works up to about 7in / 18cm tall with worsted-weight yarn and a 3.5mm hook. The proportion that makes it read as a unicorn rather than a plain horse is the size of the head relative to the body, so if you scale it, scale the head and body together rather than adding rows to just one piece. To make it bigger, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds, and one more decrease round at the end. To make it smaller, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two.

The horn doesn't need much adjustment when you resize, but keep it in proportion: on a larger unicorn, add an extra plain round to the horn's middle so it doesn't look like a little stub. On a tiny keyring-sized version, you can drop the horn down to a simple six-stitch magic ring with two plain rounds. Gifting the finished unicorn

A handmade unicorn lands differently as a gift when you tailor a couple of details to the person. For a newborn, go for the embroidered face and keep everything soft; for an older child, safety eyes and a bright mane they helped choose will mean more than a store-bought toy. One of my regulars makes the same unicorn every time but changes only the mane colour to match each new baby's nursery, and the parents now expect them by name.

If it's going to a child, sew everything on with small, tight stitches and give the seams a gentle tug test before you wrap it — hold the horn and each leg and pull lightly to be sure nothing gives. For washing, see the FAQ below, but the short version is that spot cleaning beats a machine wash for anything stuffed.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: the horn curls or leans to one side once it's sewn on.
Fix: stuff the horn firmly before attaching it, and sew it on with small stitches around the entire base, holding it upright as you work and checking it from the front and side before you tie off.
Mistake: the mane looks sparse, or strands pull out when tugged.
Fix: space the looped strands close together in a full row, and make sure each one is pulled snug through its own stitch — a latch-hook or the eye of a yarn needle makes the loop-through-loop knot far more secure than a simple sewn strand.
Mistake: the head looks lopsided or the face sits off-centre.
Fix: stuff the head evenly in small pieces, and before you add the eyes, ears, and horn, lay the piece flat and check placement from directly above — marking each spot with a scrap of yarn makes it much easier to keep everything level.
Mistake: the four legs end up at different lengths or angles, so the unicorn won't sit flat.
Fix: compare the legs side by side before sewing them on, and stuff each one to the same firmness; pin or mark the attachment points on the body first so all four sit evenly along the bottom.
Mistake: stuffing shows through gaps in the stitches.
Fix: go down a hook size so the stitches tighten up, and work the increases and decreases without pulling them loose; a smaller hook hides the stuffing far better than stuffing less firmly does.
Mistake: the ears point in different directions or end up too far back.
Fix: place both ears flat against the head before sewing anything, angled slightly forward, and check them from the front as well as the side — a tiny shift of the ears changes the whole expression.

Frequently asked questions

Is this crochet unicorn pattern safe for babies and young children?

For children under 3, embroider the face and skip plastic safety eyes, since even securely attached eyes carry a small-parts risk. The mane and horn in this pattern are made from yarn sewn or knotted onto the head, so there are no small detachable pieces a baby could pull off.

How do I make the yarn mane for the unicorn?

Cut several strands of yarn roughly 30cm long, then fold each strand in half and use a latch-hook or yarn needle to pull the looped fold through a stitch along the back of the head, drawing the cut ends through the loop to secure it. Work from between the ears down to the back of the neck, then trim the strands to a length you like.

How long does this crochet unicorn take to make?

Most beginners finish the unicorn in about 4 to 5 hours across two or three sessions, including the head, body, legs, horn, ears, and the time it takes to attach the mane and sew everything together.

Can I make the unicorn bigger or smaller?

Yes. To make it bigger, add one more increase round to the head and body before the straight rounds and one more decrease round at the end; to make it smaller, stop one increase round earlier and shorten the straight rounds by a round or two, keeping the horn and mane proportions roughly the same.

What yarn is best for an amigurumi unicorn?

A smooth worsted-weight acrylic or cotton in a light pastel shows the stitches clearly and hides nothing, so it flatters a beginner. The mane can be any yarn you have, even a fluffy or sparkly scrap, because it isn't counted as part of the shaping.

Why does my unicorn's horn curl or lean to one side?

Usually the horn was sewn on before it was stuffed or positioned straight. Stuff the horn firmly, sew it on with small stitches around the entire base, and hold it upright as you work, checking it from the front and side before you tie off.

Can I wash a finished crochet unicorn?

Spot clean with a damp cloth and mild soap. A full machine wash can mat the stuffing and loosen the mane strands, so it's best avoided unless the unicorn is made from machine-washable yarn and assembled with wash-safe materials.
