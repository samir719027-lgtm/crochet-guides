Metric, US, and UK hook sizes side by side, with the yarn weight each one is built for.

Updated 2026 · 8 min read

I once bought a hook packet labelled only "size 7," assuming it meant the same thing as the 7 I'd seen on a chart, and ended up with a hook nearly two millimetres off what I needed. The packet used old UK sizing, where the numbers count down instead of up. That one mix-up is why I now check the millimetre number on everything before it goes anywhere near a pattern, and it's the first thing I teach my students to look for. In twelve years I've watched more beginners than I can count buy the wrong hook for exactly this reason — a US letter, a UK number, and a metric size that don't line up the way anyone expects.

Safest method: always match by millimetre size first. It's the one measurement that means the same thing everywhere, regardless of which country made your hook or wrote your pattern.

Reading a hook you already own

Most modern hooks print the millimetre size directly on the handle or shaft, usually followed by the US letter in smaller text. If yours only shows a letter with no millimetre number, it's likely an older hook — check the table above by matching the letter, not the number after it, since numbering has shifted slightly between manufacturers over the decades.

Does hook material matter as much as size?

Size determines the physical dimensions of your stitches, but material shapes how the hook feels to actually use, and that affects your results too. Aluminum hooks are the most common: smooth, lightweight, and fast, with yarn sliding easily off the head. Ergonomic hooks add a thicker, often soft-grip handle that reduces hand and wrist strain over long sessions — worth considering if you crochet for hours at a time, and covered in more depth in our hooks for arthritis guide.

Wood or bamboo hooks have more texture to the surface, which grips slippery yarns like acrylic ribbon, silk blends, or bamboo yarn better than smooth metal does — useful if stitches keep sliding off your hook mid-row. None of this changes the millimetre size, but switching materials can shift your tension slightly, so it's worth a quick swatch check if you change hook material partway through a size-sensitive project.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: buying a hook by its letter or number alone.
Fix: US and UK sizing use overlapping numbers that mean different things, and even US lettering has shifted slightly between manufacturers over time. Always cross-check the millimetre size printed on the hook or packet before buying or substituting.
Mistake: assuming a bigger number always means a bigger hook.
Fix: that's true for millimetre and US sizing, but reversed for UK and old imperial sizing, where a smaller number is a larger hook. Check which system a hook or pattern uses before comparing numbers directly.
Mistake: using the "correct" hook size but still getting a different finished size than the pattern.
Fix: hook size alone doesn't guarantee gauge. Personal tension, yarn brand, and hook material all shift stitch size slightly. Crochet a small gauge swatch before starting anything where the finished size matters — see our guide to fixing project size.

Frequently asked questions

What does the letter on a crochet hook mean?

US hook sizes use a letter and number together, like H-8. The letter is the US sizing system; the number is roughly the metric size in millimetres rounded down. UK and old sizing use different numbers entirely, which is why checking the millimetre size is the safest way to compare hooks.

Is a bigger hook number a bigger hook?

In millimetre and US letter sizing, yes, a higher number means a larger hook. In UK and old imperial sizing it's reversed, a smaller number means a larger hook, which is the most common source of confusion when reading older patterns.

Why do my stitches look different from the pattern photo even with the right hook size?

Hook size alone doesn't guarantee matching gauge. Your personal tension, the yarn brand, and even the hook material can shift your stitch size. Always crochet a small gauge swatch before starting a sized project.

Does the material a hook is made from actually matter?

Yes, for comfort and yarn grip more than for the finished size. Aluminum hooks are smooth and fast, ergonomic soft-grip handles reduce hand strain on long sessions, and wood or bamboo hooks grip slippery yarn like acrylic ribbon or silk blends better than metal.

Can I use a different hook material than the pattern used and get the same result?

Yes, as long as the millimetre size matches, since material affects feel and grip rather than the physical dimensions of the hook. Some crocheters do work slightly tighter or looser depending on how slick or grippy a hook feels, so it's worth swatching if you're switching materials for a size-sensitive project.

What crochet hook size should a beginner start with?

A 5.0mm (H-8) hook with worsted-weight yarn is the most forgiving combination to learn on — it's big enough to see your stitches clearly and common enough that almost every pattern you'll try early on uses it.

Why does the same hook size feel different between brands?

Even when the millimetre size matches, the shape of the head, the taper of the throat, and the handle all vary between manufacturers, which changes how the hook feels and can nudge your tension. Swatch when switching brands for a size-sensitive project.
