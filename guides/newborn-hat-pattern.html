Free Crochet Newborn Baby Hat Pattern for Beginners

A small, soft beanie scaled down for a newborn head — a quick project for a gift or a hospital donation.

Updated 2026 · US terms · Fits 13–14in / 33–36cm head circumference

I made my first batch of these for a hospital donation drive a friend organized, thinking I'd manage two or three in an evening. I ended up making eleven, because once the crown shaping clicked I genuinely couldn't stop — each hat takes under an hour, and there's something satisfying about seeing a small stack of finished ones grow while you work through an evening of episodes on TV.

This is the smallest, quickest pattern on the whole site, and also the one where getting the small safety details right matters most. This guide covers the pattern itself plus the practical questions that come up around it: sizing for premature or larger newborns, what hospital donation programs typically expect, and the yarn choices that keep it genuinely safe for a newborn.

This hat is the smallest version of the same crown-shaping technique used across our adult beanie and toddler beanie patterns, stopped even earlier for a newborn-sized head. It's fast to finish and a popular project for hospital donation programs.

Baby safety note: use soft, machine-washable yarn with no small attachable parts such as buttons or bows, which can pose a choking hazard for infants. Many hospital donation programs specify these requirements directly. See the FAQ below for more detail.

Materials

  • 1 skein soft, machine-washable worsted-weight yarn (about 50–60 yards)
  • 4.5mm (US size 7) crochet hook
  • Stitch marker, to track the start of each round
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle, for weaving in ends

Abbreviations (US terms)

Abbr.Means
scSingle crochet
st(s)Stitch(es)
inc2 sc in same stitch (increase)
rnd(s)Round(s)

See our abbreviations glossary for the full list including UK terms.

Gauge

14 sc and 16 rows measures about 4in / 10cm square with a 4.5mm hook and worsted yarn. Fit depends more on gauge at this small size — check the flat crown circle against a newborn head circumference chart if an exact fit matters.

Notes

  • Written in US terms, worked in a continuous spiral — use a stitch marker to track round starts, same technique as our adult beanie pattern.
  • This is the smallest crown size on the site, stopping increases at round 5.
  • Use soft, machine-washable yarn without buttons, bows, or other small attachments.

Pattern

Round 1

Make a magic ring, 6 sc into ring, place marker. (6 sts)

Round 2

Inc in every st around. (12 sts)

Round 3

[sc 1, inc] around. (18 sts)

Round 4

[sc 2, inc] around. (24 sts)

Round 5

[sc 3, inc] around. (30 sts)

Rounds 6–11

Sc in every st around, no increases. Work 6 rounds straight, or until the hat measures about 1in / 2.5cm less than the newborn's head circumference. (30 sts each round)

Finishing

Slip stitch into the next stitch to close, fasten off leaving a 6in / 15cm tail. Weave in ends with a yarn needle.

Sizing for different newborns

This pattern is written for an average full-term newborn head circumference of roughly 13 to 14 inches, but not every newborn arrives at that size. For a smaller or premature baby, stop the increase rounds one round earlier (after Round 4 instead of Round 5) and reduce the straight rounds by one or two before finishing. For a larger newborn, work one additional increase round before switching to straight rounds.

If you're making hats without a specific baby in mind, a middle-of-the-road size as written works for most full-term newborns, but making a small batch across two or three sizes is genuinely useful if the hats are headed to a hospital, since NICU and general nursery babies can vary quite a bit in size.

A quick way to check fit without a baby to measure against is comparing the flat crown circle, before working the straight rounds, to a standard newborn head circumference chart or even a can of soup roughly the right diameter. It's not exact, but it catches an obviously too-small or too-large crown before you've invested time in the straight rounds and finishing.

Donating to a hospital

Check the specific requirements of the program you're donating to before starting a batch, since requirements vary more than people expect — some request particular colors, others specify a maximum or minimum size, and most have firm rules about yarn type and any decorative additions. A quick email or phone call to the hospital's volunteer or NICU department before you start crocheting saves the disappointment of a finished batch that doesn't meet their guidelines.

Gender-neutral colors like white, yellow, green, or soft grey are the safest default when you don't know the specifics, since many programs give hats out before a baby's sex is announced. Batching production helps too — once the pattern is memorized, working several in a row in slightly different colors is faster than making one hat at a time and picking it back up later.

It's also worth asking whether the program has a preferred way to receive donations — some want hats mailed in, others prefer them dropped off with a volunteer coordinator, and a few only accept donations through an organized drive rather than individual contributions. A short conversation up front avoids a batch of finished hats sitting in a drawer because the delivery logistics weren't sorted out first.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: using a fuzzy or novelty yarn that looks cute but sheds loose fibers near a newborn's face.
Fix: stick to a smooth, soft acrylic or cotton yarn — it's the safer standard for anything worn close to a baby's face.
Mistake: adding a small decorative button, bow, or flower for a cuter look, without realizing it's a choking hazard.
Fix: keep any decoration crocheted directly into the fabric rather than sewn on as a separate small piece, or skip decoration entirely — plain is standard for newborn hats for good reason.
Mistake: making a whole batch of hats before checking a specific hospital program's size, color, or yarn requirements.
Fix: confirm requirements first, even for a program you've donated to before, since guidelines can change between donation drives.
Mistake: working too loosely on such a small, fast project, resulting in a hat that looks finished but stretches out of shape quickly.
Fix: keep tension even and check gauge even on a small project like this — it's easy to rush through six quick rounds without paying attention to consistency.

Frequently asked questions

What size head does this newborn hat fit?

This pattern is sized for an average newborn head circumference, roughly 13 to 14 inches. Premature or larger newborns will need a smaller or larger crown, adjusted by stopping the increase rounds one round earlier or later.

What yarn is safe for newborn hats donated to hospitals?

Many hospital donation programs require soft, machine-washable acrylic or cotton yarn without any small attachable parts like buttons or bows, since these can be a choking hazard for infants.

How long does a newborn hat take to crochet?

Given the small size, most beginners finish a newborn hat in about an hour, making it a quick, meaningful project for hospital donation programs or new parent gifts.

How many hats do hospitals usually want per donation?

This varies widely by program, so it's worth contacting the specific hospital or organization first; some accept single hats, while others prefer batches of a dozen or more delivered at once.

Are novelty or fuzzy yarns okay to use for a newborn hat?

It's best to avoid mohair, eyelash, or other fuzzy novelty yarns for newborn items, since loose fibers can shed near a baby's face; a smooth, soft acrylic or cotton is the safer standard choice.

What colors do hospitals typically prefer for donated newborn hats?

Many programs prefer gender-neutral colors like white, yellow, green, or soft grey, since hats are often given out before a baby's sex is confirmed or announced, but this varies by hospital and it's worth checking their specific preference.
