Crochet Abbreviations Glossary: US & UK Terms

Pin this pattern

The same words mean different stitches in each system — here's every common abbreviation, side by side.

Updated 2026 · 5 min read

Crochet abbreviations get confusing fast once you realise "dc" doesn't mean the same thing everywhere. The US and UK use identical letters for different stitches, offset by one step — a mismatch that trips up even experienced crocheters switching between pattern sources.

The one thing to remember: every UK term is offset by one stitch from its US equivalent. UK double crochet (dc) is US single crochet (sc). Once that clicks, the whole system falls into place.

How to tell which system a pattern uses

Most published patterns state this near the top, often as "US terms" or "UK terms" in the materials list. If it isn't stated, the fastest check is looking for a dc stitch and seeing how tall it's described as — a short, dense stitch means US terms, while a taller stitch means UK terms.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between US and UK crochet terms?

The two systems use the same words for different stitches, offset by one. What the US calls single crochet (sc), the UK calls double crochet (dc). Checking which system a pattern uses before starting prevents your stitch count and fabric height from being wrong throughout the entire project.

How do I know if a pattern is written in US or UK terms?

Most patterns state it near the top, often as "US terms" or "UK terms." If it isn't stated, check the designer's country or the source website, and look for a stitch called "dc" used for a small, tight stitch (UK) versus a taller one (US).

Do stitch abbreviations mean the same thing in every pattern?

Within one terminology system, yes, standard abbreviations like ch, sc, and dc are consistent. Some designers invent their own shorthand for unusual stitches, so always check a pattern's own abbreviations key if one is provided.
