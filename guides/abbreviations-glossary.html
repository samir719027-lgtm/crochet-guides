Crochet Abbreviations Glossary: US & UK Terms

Pin this pattern

The same words mean different stitches in each system — here's every common abbreviation, side by side.

Updated 2026 · 8 min read

A reader once messaged me confused that her "double crochet" scarf looked nothing like the tight, dense fabric in my photos — and it turned out she was using a UK pattern with US instructions layered over it in her head, so every "dc" she read was quietly the wrong stitch. Untangling that for her is what convinced me this glossary needed to exist on its own, not buried as a footnote in other guides.

Crochet abbreviations get confusing fast once you realise "dc" doesn't mean the same thing everywhere. The US and UK use identical letters for different stitches, offset by one step — a mismatch that trips up even experienced crocheters switching between pattern sources.

The one thing to remember: every UK term is offset by one stitch from its US equivalent. UK double crochet (dc) is US single crochet (sc). Once that clicks, the whole system falls into place.

How to tell which system a pattern uses

Most published patterns state this near the top, often as "US terms" or "UK terms" in the materials list. If it isn't stated, the fastest check is looking for a dc stitch and seeing how tall it's described as — a short, dense stitch means US terms, while a taller stitch means UK terms.

Reading pattern notation beyond single stitches

Abbreviations tell you what each stitch is, but patterns also use symbols to compress repeated sequences instead of writing them out longhand. An asterisk (*) marks the start of a repeat, closed later with "rep from * around" or "rep from * to end" — everything between the asterisk and that instruction repeats as many times as the pattern needs. Square brackets group a short sequence with a repeat count right after them, like [sc 2, inc] x6, meaning "work single crochet 2, then an increase, and do that whole group six times."

A number in parentheses at the end of a row or round, like (24 sts), is your stitch-count checkpoint — not an instruction to follow, but a total to check your own count against before moving to the next row.

Common mistakes & fixes

Mistake: working a whole project in the wrong stitch because "dc" was read as the wrong system.
Fix: check the pattern's stated terminology before starting, and if it's not stated, compare the described stitch height against the abbreviation — a short dc is US terms, a tall one is UK.
Mistake: misreading a bracketed repeat and working the sequence the wrong number of times.
Fix: read the full bracket plus its repeat count before starting the round, not stitch by stitch, and check your total against the stitch count in parentheses at the end of the row to catch a miscount immediately.
Mistake: assuming a designer's custom shorthand matches standard abbreviations.
Fix: most standard abbreviations are consistent within one terminology system, but some designers invent shorthand for unusual or decorative stitches. Always check a pattern's own abbreviations key at the top before assuming.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between US and UK crochet terms?

The two systems use the same words for different stitches, offset by one. What the US calls single crochet (sc), the UK calls double crochet (dc). Checking which system a pattern uses before starting prevents your stitch count and fabric height from being wrong throughout the entire project.

How do I know if a pattern is written in US or UK terms?

Most patterns state it near the top, often as "US terms" or "UK terms." If it isn't stated, check the designer's country or the source website, and look for a stitch called "dc" used for a small, tight stitch (UK) versus a taller one (US).

Do stitch abbreviations mean the same thing in every pattern?

Within one terminology system, yes, standard abbreviations like ch, sc, and dc are consistent. Some designers invent their own shorthand for unusual stitches, so always check a pattern's own abbreviations key if one is provided.

What do asterisks and brackets mean in a crochet pattern?

They mark a repeating sequence rather than a stitch. An asterisk pairs with "rep from *" to mark where a repeat starts, and square brackets group a sequence with a repeat count after them, like [sc 2, inc] x6, meaning that whole bracketed sequence six times.

What does the number in parentheses at the end of a row mean?

It's the total stitch count you should have once that row or round is complete, used to check your work before moving on. If your count doesn't match, it's worth recounting that row before continuing rather than assuming it will even out later.
