How to Read a Crochet Pattern Without Panicking

Pin this pattern

Abbreviations, gauge, and repeats explained the way we wish someone had explained them to us.

Updated 2026 · 10 min read

I still remember printing out my first real pattern, a simple hat, and staring at the first line for a full five minutes because it might as well have been written in another language: rows of abbreviations, asterisks, and numbers stacked in brackets. Nobody had told me that a pattern is really just a recipe, written as compactly as the author could manage, and that the shorthand exists to save space, not to gatekeep beginners. Once I understood that, the panic mostly went away, and I started actually reading patterns instead of just staring at them.

This guide covers the specific pieces that trip people up most: the abbreviations themselves, what gauge is actually measuring, how to read a repeat, and how to catch a mistake early by checking your stitch count as you go.

Quick answer: read the pattern fully once before picking up your hook. Most confusion comes from starting mid-row on a stitch you haven't seen defined yet.

Before you pick up the hook

Every pattern worth using has a short block of notes before the instructions start, and it's the part almost everyone skips. It usually covers three things worth reading first: which terms it uses (US or UK — see the FAQ below if you're not sure which you're looking at), any special stitches or abbreviations unique to that pattern that aren't in the standard list, and the finished size or gauge the designer worked to. Skipping this section is the single most common reason a pattern feels confusing five minutes in — you're usually missing a definition the designer assumed you'd already seen.

What gauge actually means

Gauge tells you how many stitches and rows fit into a set square, usually 10cm/4in, when using the hook and yarn the pattern recommends. It exists because everyone's tension is slightly different — two people using the same hook and yarn can produce noticeably different-sized results.

For anything where exact size matters (garments, amigurumi with fitted parts), crochet a small gauge swatch first. For anything forgiving (scarves, blankets), you can usually skip it.

Reading repeats: asterisks and brackets

When you see something like:

*sc in next st, ch 1, skip 1 st; rep from * to end

Everything between the asterisks is one repeating unit. You work it over and over until you reach the end of the row, rather than the pattern spelling out the same instruction fifteen times.

Brackets work the same way but usually with a stated repeat count, like [dc, ch 1] x4 — do the bracketed steps exactly four times.

Reading a stitch count

Most rows end with a number in parentheses, like (12 sts). This tells you how many stitches you should have after finishing the row. Checking this every row — not just at the end of a project — is the fastest way to catch a missed stitch before it compounds.

Common reading mistakes

Mixing up US and UK terms mid-project. This is the most common and most frustrating mistake, because both versions look identical on the page — only the stitch names swap meaning. If your gauge swatch looks noticeably taller or shorter than expected, check the pattern's terms before assuming your tension is off.
Not counting stitches until something already looks wrong. By the time a mistake is visible, it's often 10-15 rows back. Get in the habit of checking the stitch count in parentheses at the end of every row while you're still learning a new pattern.
Skipping the pattern notes at the top. Special stitches, sizing notes, and which terms are in use almost always live in a short block before Row 1. Reading it first saves far more time than it costs.

Frequently asked questions

What does gauge mean in a crochet pattern?

Gauge tells you how many stitches and rows should fit into a set measurement, usually 10cm or 4 inches, when using the recommended hook and yarn. Matching it ensures your finished piece comes out the size the pattern intends.

What do the asterisks and brackets in a pattern mean?

Asterisks and brackets mark a section that repeats. Everything between the marks is worked as many times as stated, so you don't have to read the same instruction written out over and over.

Why doesn't my stitch count match the pattern?

The most common cause is a missed or extra stitch at the start or end of a row. Count your stitches at the end of every row while you're learning, rather than waiting until the end of the project to check.

What's the difference between US and UK crochet pattern terms?

The same stitch name means a different stitch in each system, most famously UK "double crochet" being the same stitch as US "single crochet." Always check which terms a pattern uses before you start, since working the wrong stitch height throughout a project is a very easy mistake to make.

What is a turning chain and why does it matter?

A turning chain is one or more chain stitches worked at the start of a new row to bring your hook up to the correct height for that row's stitch. Depending on the stitch, that turning chain may or may not count as your first stitch of the row, and getting that wrong is one of the most common causes of a stitch count drifting off over several rows.
